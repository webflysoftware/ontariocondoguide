In June 2026, owners at one of Miami’s largest condominiums watched a board election stretch past midnight. According to reporting by the *Miami Herald*, a state election monitor spent hours verifying signatures, Facetimed owners to confirm whether they had actually mailed their ballots, tossed disputed votes, and documented forgeries and other irregularities. The longtime board president lost his seat.

The details are local. The lesson is not.

Association elections rarely make national news. When they do, it is usually because trust has already collapsed — and because the community skipped the boring safeguards that keep a routine vote from becoming a forensic investigation.

## Integrity is a process, not a slogan

Boards often treat ballot security as a paperwork afterthought: collect envelopes, open them at the meeting, announce winners. That works until someone asks a hard question.

The Miami coverage described ballots with matching handwriting across multiple owners, signatures that did not match, and batches of ballots arriving together with the same postmark. Whether every allegation is ultimately proven in court is beside the point for other associations. Once owners believe the count is theater, the result does not settle the fight — it starts the next one.

**Lesson:** Design the process so a skeptical owner can watch it and still believe the outcome.

## Signature verification is not optional theater

If your association uses mailed ballots or limited proxies, signature comparison is often the only practical check against forgery. Skipping it because “we have never had a problem” is how problems become scandals.

Practical habits that reduce risk:

- Keep a current owner signature specimen file (closing docs, prior ballots, or a signed owner directory form)
- Use outer envelopes that identify the unit without revealing the vote
- Have inspectors of election — not candidates — open and validate ballots
- Log every rejected ballot with a reason
- Preserve ballots and envelopes for the retention period in your documents or state law

When verification is rushed or done by people with a stake in the outcome, you invite exactly the challenge you hoped to avoid.

## Independent eyes calm rooms

Owners in the Miami case organized a petition for a state monitor. That option is not available everywhere, but the instinct is sound: bring in someone with no slate to protect.

Even without a state program, boards can:

- Hire a professional election services firm
- Appoint inspectors of election who are not running
- Allow candidate observers at the count
- Publish the counting rules *before* ballots go out

Transparency is cheaper than litigation. It is also faster than rebuilding trust after a contested midnight count.

## Proxies and project votes deserve the same rigor

Reporting around the same community also focused on a limited proxy vote tied to a capital project and bylaw change. That pattern is familiar: boards treat director elections as “official” and special votes as informal paperwork.

Owners do not see the distinction. A forged or unauthorized proxy on a pool renovation feels the same as a forged director ballot — because both decide how their money and building are run.

**Lesson:** Apply one integrity standard to every membership vote. See [Proxy Voting Basics](/guides/proxy-voting-basics).

## What boards should do before the next election

1. Write down the ballot chain of custody from mailing to certification
2. Name inspectors of election early and put their duties in the notice package
3. Confirm whether your state allows an election monitor or complaint process — and tell owners how it works
4. Run a dry-run count with last year’s blank forms so the real night is not improvisation
5. Use the [Election Checklist](/templates/election-checklist) and [Election Readiness Check](/tools/election-readiness-check)

## What owners should insist on

Ask for the election rules in writing. Ask who verifies signatures. Ask how rejected ballots are logged. Ask whether candidates or their agents handle envelopes. If the answers are vague, push for clarity *before* ballots go out — not after results are announced.

## The real takeaway

Most associations will never face a state monitor or a newspaper investigation. The ones that do usually spent years treating elections as a formality.

Ballot integrity is not about assuming bad faith. It is about building a process that still works when someone *does* act in bad faith — or when an honest mistake looks like one. Communities that get this right rarely make the news. That is the goal.
