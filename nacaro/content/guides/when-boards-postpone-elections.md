In early June 2026, News 6 in Orlando reported that the North Shore at Lake Hart HOA had “postponed” its annual board elections for a second straight year. Residents showed up anyway. The meeting adjourned for lack of quorum. The existing board stayed in place while an appeal of a court-confirmed arbitration order worked its way through the system.

Different community, familiar pattern: elections delayed, owners frustrated, legitimacy questioned.

This piece is not a verdict on that lawsuit. It is a lesson about what happens when voting becomes optional for the people already in power.

## Elections are not a board courtesy

In a membership corporation or association, the annual election is not a social event the board may reschedule when politics get uncomfortable. It is how owners authorize the people who spend their assessments, hire the manager, and enforce the rules.

When a board repeatedly announces that elections are “deferred until the case makes its way through the courts,” owners hear something simpler: *You do not get a vote until we say so.*

Even if counsel believes a stay or appeal justifies delay, the governance cost is real. Neighbors stop volunteering. Participation collapses. Every budget decision looks like self-dealing — fair or not — because the board’s mandate is stale.

## Litigation is not a blank check to stay in office

Disputes over candidate eligibility, bylaw amendments, and prior election defects are common. Florida’s DBPR arbitration system exists precisely because those fights need a forum short of full civil trial.

What associations get wrong is treating an adverse order as something to outlast rather than something to comply with or narrowly stay. Appealing a remedy is one thing. Using years of process to avoid seating a newly elected board is another — and owners notice.

**Opinion:** If a tribunal ordered a new election, the default posture should be *how do we run a clean election under that order?* not *how long can we keep the current roster?*

## Quorum theater makes it worse

Adjournment for lack of quorum after announcing that elections will not occur is a special kind of failure. Owners who still show up are performing civic duty in a system that has already told them it will not count.

Boards in this position should either:

- Hold the election the documents require, or
- Obtain a clear court order authorizing delay and communicate that order, with dates and legal basis, in plain language

Vague postponements breed conspiracy theories. Specific, documented legal constraints at least give owners a reason that is not “trust us.”

## Candidate-disqualification rules cut both ways

Many of these fights start with bylaws that bar owners in litigation with the association from running for the board. Sometimes the rule is meant to prevent conflicts. Sometimes it looks like incumbents fencing out critics.

Either way, rewriting eligibility rules mid-conflict is how routine elections become multi-year arbitration sagas. If your documents need clearer candidate standards, amend them through the proper owner vote — not as an emergency shield for the sitting board.

## What responsible boards do under legal pressure

1. Ask counsel for a written memo: what the order requires, what is stayed, and what election obligations remain
2. Publish that memo (or a clear summary) to owners
3. Separate “we disagree with the ruling” from “owners may not elect directors”
4. Keep meeting minutes, budgets, and vendor decisions unusually transparent while elections are in limbo
5. Set a public target date for the next owner vote, even if it is contingent on a court ruling

Directors still owe fiduciary duties while litigation is pending. See [Board Director Duties](/guides/board-director-duties).

## What owners can push for

- Written explanation of each postponement, citing the order or statute relied on
- Confirmation of which seats are expired, appointed, or holdover
- A timeline for the next election once the stay or appeal is resolved
- Administrative or court enforcement if the association ignores a final order

Owner fatigue is part of the strategy in prolonged election fights. Persistence, documentation, and collective petitions matter more than one angry email.

## Bottom line

Communities can survive a contested election. They struggle to survive *no* election.

Boards that treat voting as a risk to manage rather than a duty to perform may win another year in office — and lose the community’s consent to be governed. That trade is almost never worth it.
