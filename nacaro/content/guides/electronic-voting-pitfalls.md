In March 2026, a Kaufman County, Texas court froze the Devonshire Residential Association’s board election. According to local reporting and the homeowner’s lawsuit, owners faced conflicting ballot deadlines, a new email-dependent voting system with bounce-backs and broken links, and uneven notice that left some residents with little or no chance to participate. The temporary restraining order blocked certification of results and required preservation of electronic logs and bounce-back records.

Online voting was supposed to make participation easier. Instead, it became the exhibit list.

## Digital is not automatically fairer

Paper elections fail in familiar ways: late mail, lost proxies, sloppy registration. Electronic elections fail in newer ways that boards underestimate:

- Invitation emails land in spam
- Portal links expire or 404
- “Owner of record” email lists are years out of date
- One system says voting closes at 11:30 a.m.; the printed packet says noon
- Staff tell callers the process will restart while the candidate window stays closed

Each of those is a notice problem dressed up as a tech problem. Courts care about whether owners received a fair, uniform opportunity to vote — not whether the vendor’s dashboard looked modern.

## Conflicting deadlines are an unforced error

If two official communications disagree about when voting ends, you have already given a challenger a clean argument. It does not matter which deadline you *meant*.

**Rule of thumb:** One election calendar, published once, repeated everywhere — email, portal, paper packet, website, and door notice. If you must correct a deadline, issue a single amended notice to *all* owners and extend time rather than shorten it.

Use the [Meeting Notice Checklist](/templates/meeting-notice-checklist) even when the “meeting” is mostly electronic.

## Email-gated voting excludes people by design

Requiring an email invite to vote is convenient for managers and hostile to owners who:

- Never opted into electronic notice
- Changed providers and never updated the association
- Share a unit among family members with one outdated address on file
- Do not monitor email daily during a short voting window

If your documents or state law still require mail notice — or allow owners to refuse electronic delivery — an email-only ballot is a liability. Hybrid delivery (mail notice + electronic ballot option) is slower and safer.

## Bounce-backs are evidence, not IT trivia

The Texas court ordered preservation of email bounce-back logs. That should tell every board something: delivery failure is part of the election record.

Before certifying results, ask:

- How many election emails bounced?
- Were those owners contacted another way?
- Did anyone vote using a link that later proved broken?
- Can you produce a unit-by-unit notice log?

If you cannot answer those questions, you are not ready to announce winners.

## Vendor transitions mid-election are reckless

Rolling out a new communication or voting platform in the same cycle as director elections is a classic self-inflicted wound. Test the system in a low-stakes survey months earlier. Keep the prior channel available as backup. Do not learn about spam filters on election week.

## A practical electronic-election checklist

1. Confirm electronic/absentee voting is authorized by statute *and* your documents
2. Meet the longest applicable notice period (document vs. statute — use the stricter one)
3. Publish one deadline set; ban informal “clarifications” that change it
4. Mail a paper notice even if ballots are electronic, unless every owner has a valid e-notice consent on file
5. Track opens, bounces, and help-desk tickets daily during the voting window
6. Appoint inspectors of election who can access vendor audit logs
7. Preserve everything until the challenge period expires

Pair this with [Board Election Procedures](/guides/board-election-procedures) and the [Election Readiness Check](/tools/election-readiness-check).

## Opinion: convenience is not consent

Associations adopt electronic voting to raise turnout and cut printing costs. Those are good goals. They do not override the older duty: every owner entitled to vote must have a real chance to do so under clear, consistent rules.

When a court freezes an election, the association does not merely lose a news cycle. It loses months of governance certainty, pays counsel on both sides of a TRO, and teaches owners that the process was never under control.

Run the technology like an election — because that is what it is.
